Predicting Phytoplankton Absorption Directly from Satellite TOA Radiance

Bypassing atmospheric correction with Bayesian models

Summary

This study revisits an idea first explored in our 2019 preprint on direct retrieval of phytoplankton absorption (\(a_{\phi}\)) from top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiance.
In that earlier work (Craig & Karaköylü, 2019), we demonstrated that probabilistic machine learning could bypass full atmospheric correction (AC) by learning the relationship between TOA radiance and in-water optical properties.

This new version updates that concept using a fully Bayesian workflow, modern PyMC v5 implementations, and a set of progressively richer models to test what model structure most faithfully captures the data-generating process.

Why This Matters

Atmospheric correction often fails in optically complex coastal and inland waters, where the standard assumptions (e.g., dark NIR, co-varying constituents) are violated.
These failures cause large retrieval errors and data gaps in exactly the ecosystems where biogeochemical information is most needed.

This work explores a Bayesian alternative: directly linking TOA radiance (after Rayleigh and Fresnel corrections) to phytoplankton absorption without subtracting the atmospheric contribution.
By modeling uncertainty explicitly, we gain calibrated interval estimates and insight into how well each model generalizes to unseen data.

Data

  • Dataset: Modified NOMAD–SeaWiFS matchup dataset (Werdell & Bailey 2005).
  • Predictors: TOA radiance at 412, 443, 490, 510, 555, 670 nm (log-transformed).
  • Targets: \(a_{\phi}\) at 443, 555, 670 nm (log-transformed).
  • Sample size: 163 collocated matchups.

No feature engineering or PCA was applied — only logarithmic transformation to stabilize variance and maintain interpretability.

Model Progression

We developed four Bayesian models of increasing structural richness:

  1. Linear model — Baseline regression with diagonal covariance (independent errors).
  2. Hierarchical linear model — Partial pooling across absorption bands to share statistical strength.
  3. Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) — Nonlinear mean, diagonal covariance.
  4. Multivariate linear model (MVN) — Linear mean with a full covariance matrix across bands.

The first three models used independent error terms to isolate the effect of the mean-function structure.
We then introduced a full covariance structure in the fourth model to explicitly capture cross-band residual correlations, a physically realistic feature of ocean-color retrievals.

The non-hierarchical form was chosen for the covariance test to isolate the effect of \(\Sigma\)-structure alone, avoiding confounding between group pooling and covariance learning.

Results Overview

All covariance-enabled models improved calibration and coverage.
Among them, the multivariate linear model achieved the best predictive performance and near-nominal \(94\%\) coverage, showing that properly modeling covariance outweighed mean-function complexity.

Takeaway:
The best-performing model wasn’t the most flexible, but the one most faithful to the data-generating process — capturing how \(a_{\phi}\) bands covary in nature.
